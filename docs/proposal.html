01Abstract

Verity is a sharded, linearizable, replicated key-value store written from first principles in Go. It has three layers: a complete Raft consensus implementation, a multi-group sharding layer with online shard migration, and — the distinguishing contribution — a deterministic simulation testing harness in which the entire cluster executes inside a single-threaded, seeded simulator with virtual time.

Inside the simulator, every source of nondeterminism is drawn from one 64-bit seed: message delay, drop, duplication and reordering; network partitions of arbitrary topology; node crashes and restarts; disk latency, torn writes, and data loss on crash-without-fsync; and clock skew. Because time is virtual, one simulated hour costs milliseconds of real CPU, and because everything is seeded, any failure the harness finds is reproducible from a single integer — forever, on any machine.

The project therefore inverts the usual emphasis. The datastore is the system under test; the testing methodology is the research artifact. Correctness is reported as a measurement — linearizability violations found by an external checker across hundreds of thousands of simulated cluster-hours — and the effectiveness of the methodology is itself measured, by a controlled bug-injection study comparing deterministic simulation against conventional testing at equal CPU cost.

02Problem statement and motivation

2.1 Distributed systems fail where tests do not look

A replicated system is correct only if it behaves correctly in every interleaving of message ordering, partial failure, and timing. The defects that matter therefore live in rare schedules: a leader that is partitioned but does not yet know it; a follower whose disk lost the last un-fsynced write; two configuration changes that overlap by one term. These are not found by unit tests, because a unit test fixes the schedule.

Conventional integration testing runs the real system, on a real network, in real time. That approach samples a vanishingly small fraction of the schedule space, and — critically — samples it non-reproducibly. A test that fails once in five hundred runs is, in practice, undebuggable: by the time the engineer looks, the schedule that produced it is gone. Most such failures are quietly re-run until green.

2.2 Correctness is a global property

Linearizability, the consistency model Verity targets, is defined over the history of a concurrent execution: every operation must appear to take effect atomically at some instant between its invocation and its response, consistent with real-time order. No node can check this from the inside — it is a property of the whole system's observable history, and deciding it is NP-complete in general. Correctness therefore has to be checked externally, over a recorded history, by a dedicated checker.

2.3 What industry does, and what students do not

Industrial practice has converged on two answers. The first is external black-box fault injection, popularised by Jepsen, which drives a real cluster while a separate process induces partitions and clock skew and then checks the resulting history. The second, far more powerful, is deterministic simulation: run the entire system in a single process, under a virtual clock, with every I/O and every random choice supplied by a seeded generator. FoundationDB pioneered the technique, TigerBeetle built its whole engineering culture on it, and Antithesis now sells it as a deterministic hypervisor.

Simulation is dramatically more effective and remains, outside a handful of teams, poorly understood. Student and small-team distributed systems are almost universally validated by ad-hoc testing and a hopeful demo. That gap is the opportunity this project takes.

Thesis in one sentence

A single developer, in seven months, can build a non-trivial sharded consensus system and produce stronger evidence of its correctness than conventional testing can supply — provided the simulator is built before the system, not after it.

03Objectives and claimed contributions

3.1 Primary objectives

P1
Implement Raft from the specification, including the parts routinely omitted from teaching implementations: durable state, log compaction and snapshot transfer, single-server membership change, PreVote, and leader leases.
P2
Build a linearizable key-value service across multiple Raft groups, with a replicated shard controller, online shard migration under live write load, and exactly-once client semantics.
P3
Build a deterministic simulator with virtual time and seeded fault injection, under which the unmodified system runs, and in which every failure is replayable from a 64-bit seed.
P4
Evaluate empirically along three axes — correctness under fault, performance against a production baseline (etcd), and availability during failure and reconfiguration.

3.2 Secondary objectives (pursued if schedule allows)

S1
Automatic minimisation of failing traces: on failure, replay the seed with faults progressively removed to produce the smallest reproducer.
S2
Swarm-style seed exploration, in which the fault configuration is itself randomised per seed, so that no single "average" fault mix masks a class of bug.
S3
A TLA+ model of the shard-migration protocol, model-checked with TLC over small configurations, as a cross-check on the hand-written implementation.

3.3 Claimed contributions

What a reader or examiner should be able to take from the finished work:

C1
An open-source, simulation-tested Raft and multi-Raft implementation of a completeness rarely reached outside production codebases.
C2
A reusable deterministic simulation harness for Go distributed systems, published as a standalone package independent of Verity itself.
C3
A controlled empirical comparison of deterministic simulation against conventional testing, via systematic injection of known consensus bugs, reporting detection rate and time-to-first-failure at equal CPU cost. This is the study that makes the work a piece of research rather than an implementation exercise.
C4
An empirical characterisation of leader-lease read safety under clock skew: the measured skew threshold at which lease-based reads begin to violate linearizability, and confirmation that ReadIndex-based reads do not.

04Scope and non-goals

Bounding the work is as important as defining it. The following are deliberately and permanently excluded; several are named because they are the directions a reader is most likely to ask about.

  • Byzantine fault tolerance — crash-recovery model only
  • Multi-key or cross-shard ACID transactions
  • SQL layer, secondary indexes, range scans across shards
  • WAN-optimised or leaderless consensus (EPaxos, Paxos variants)
  • Custom network transport or kernel bypass
  • Authentication, TLS, multi-tenancy, quota enforcement
  • Production operations tooling, dashboards, or a management UI
  • Machine-checked proof of the full system (S3 is a bounded model, not a proof)

Cross-shard transactions are the most natural extension and will be discussed as future work, with the observation that Verity's shard-migration machinery already supplies most of what a two-phase commit coordinator would need.

05System architecture

5.1 Overview

Verity is five components. A client library routes each request by key to the replica group that currently owns its shard, caching the shard map and retrying on redirection. A shard controller — itself a small Raft group — owns the authoritative mapping from shards to groups, versioned by a monotonic configuration number. Several replica groups, each a Raft group of three or five nodes, own disjoint subsets of shards and apply committed commands to a local state machine. A storage layer gives each node a write-ahead log and periodic snapshots. And the simulator encloses all of it, supplying time, network, disk, and randomness.

5.2 Layer one — the consensus core

Raft is implemented to the specification and its dissertation extensions, not the abridged version. In addition to leader election, log replication, and durable term/vote/log state fsynced before any response, the implementation includes:

  • PreVote. A node partitioned away and repeatedly timing out inflates its term; on rejoin it deposes a perfectly healthy leader. PreVote requires a candidate to win a straw poll before incrementing its term, removing the disruption.
  • Term-based conflict backtracking. Rolling nextIndex back one entry at a time is O(n) round trips after a long divergence; the follower instead reports the first index of the conflicting term so the leader can skip a whole term per round trip.
  • Batching and pipelining. Multiple client commands per AppendEntries, and multiple in-flight AppendEntries per follower, without waiting for acknowledgement. This is where most of the throughput lives.
  • Snapshots and InstallSnapshot. Log compaction above a size threshold, plus transfer of a snapshot to a follower whose required entry has already been discarded.
  • Single-server membership change. The one-at-a-time approach from Raft §4, chosen over joint consensus because it is materially simpler to get right and sufficient for the reconfiguration Verity needs.
  • The Figure 8 commit restriction. A leader may only mark an entry committed once it has replicated an entry of its own term; committing a replicated-but-earlier-term entry by counting replicas alone can be undone. This is the single most commonly mis-implemented rule in Raft, and the simulator will be pointed at it deliberately in the bug-injection study.

Linearizable reads without writing to the log

A read served naively by the leader can return stale data, because the leader may have been deposed without knowing it. Appending every read to the log is correct but expensive. Verity implements both cheap alternatives, and this turns out to be experimentally interesting:

  • ReadIndex. The leader records its current commit index, confirms leadership by a round of heartbeats to a quorum, waits for its state machine to apply up to that index, and serves the read. Costs one round trip; safe under any clock behaviour.
  • Leader lease. The leader assumes it remains leader for a bounded interval after a successful heartbeat round and serves reads locally with no round trip. Faster — but safe only if clock drift between nodes stays inside an assumed bound.

That second assumption is exactly the kind of thing a simulator can attack, and doing so is research question RQ5 in §7.

5.3 Layer two — sharding and online migration

The key space is divided into a fixed number of shards (1024) hashed from the key. The shard controller — a three-node Raft group of its own — holds a sequence of numbered configurations, each mapping shards to replica groups. Groups joining or leaving trigger a rebalance, which produces a new configuration and therefore a set of shards that must move.

Migration proceeds per shard: the source group commits a "freeze" entry through its own Raft log and stops serving the shard; it ships the shard's state together with the client deduplication table for keys in that shard; the destination group commits an "install" entry; the source commits a garbage-collection entry once installation is acknowledged. Every step goes through consensus, so a leader failure on either side mid-migration resumes rather than corrupts.

Subtle failure this design defends against

If the deduplication table does not move with the shard, exactly-once semantics silently break. A client whose request was applied by the source group, but whose response was lost, retries against the destination group — which has no record of it and applies it a second time. An increment becomes two increments. No test that omits migration-under-retry will ever see this, and no node observes anything anomalous. It is precisely the class of bug the simulator exists to find.

5.4 Layer three — deterministic simulation

The simulator replaces the runtime, not the system. Four rules make a run reproducible, and all four constrain the code from the first commit:

  • No wall clock. Time is a counter advanced by the scheduler to the timestamp of the next event. There are no real timers and no sleeping; a simulated hour of an idle cluster costs almost nothing.
  • No concurrent scheduling. Nodes are not goroutines. Each node is a state machine exposing Step(event) []Action — it consumes an event, mutates its state, and returns the messages and disk writes it wants performed. The simulator owns the single thread and decides what happens next.
  • No real I/O. The network is an in-memory model with a delay distribution, drop and duplication rates, reordering, and an arbitrary — possibly asymmetric — partition topology. The disk is a model with write latency, explicit fsync semantics, torn writes, and loss of un-fsynced data on simulated crash.
  • One seed, one trace. Every random choice in the system and the harness comes from one seeded source. Same seed, same binary, same trace — byte for byte, on any machine.
The one decision that cannot be deferred

Verity must be written simulation-first. Time, network, disk, and randomness pass through injected interfaces from the very first commit, and the harness is built in month one — before any consensus code exists. Retrofitting determinism onto a codebase already structured around goroutines, channels, and time.Now() is not a refactor; it is a rewrite. Every schedule risk in §9 flows from this decision being taken correctly at the start.

06Technology and implementation approach

6.1 Language

Go 1.26. The choice is driven by the simulator, not by preference. The Step(event) []Action architecture that determinism demands is the same structure etcd's raft library already uses, so there is a high-quality reference for the shape of the code. Porcupine, the linearizability checker, is a Go library and integrates directly. etcd itself is written in Go, which makes the performance comparison in RQ3 an honest like-for-like rather than a language benchmark. And the toolchain supplies race detection, coverage-guided fuzzing, and profiling without third-party infrastructure.

Rust was considered and rejected: the borrow checker fights the shared-mutable shape of a Raft node hard enough to cost weeks that this schedule does not have, and no part of the project benefits from what that cost buys.

6.3 Determinism hygiene

Determinism is fragile and fails silently. Three practices defend it, enforced in continuous integration from month one: every seed is executed twice and the resulting event traces diffed; iteration over Go maps is banned on any deterministic path in favour of sorted key order, because Go deliberately randomises map iteration; and time.Now(), math/rand's global source, and direct filesystem and network calls are forbidden outside the injected interfaces, checked by a custom lint rule.

07Evaluation methodology

Five research questions, each with a stated metric and a stated success criterion. Every figure in the thesis is regenerated from raw data by a script committed alongside it; every experiment is repeated at least three times and reported with variance, not means alone.

RQ1Is Verity linearizable under fault?

The system is driven by a concurrent workload while the simulator injects the full fault catalogue. Every resulting history is submitted to Porcupine. Separately, the implementation is run against the independent 6.5840 suite, and the real five-node deployment is subjected to tc netem partitions.

Metric
Linearizability violations per 103 simulated cluster-hours, broken down by fault class.
Success
≥ 105 simulated cluster-hours with zero unresolved violations at submission; independent suite passing; every violation found during development documented in the bug catalogue with its seed.

RQ2Does deterministic simulation find bugs that conventional testing misses?

The central study. A catalogue of known consensus defects is injected one at a time into an otherwise-correct build — omitting the Figure 8 commit restriction; failing to persist votedFor before responding; acknowledging a write before fsync; serving a shard under a stale configuration; migrating a shard without its deduplication table. Each mutant is given to both regimes at equal CPU cost: the deterministic harness, and a conventional suite of unit tests plus real-network stress runs.

The second half of the study is observational: every genuine bug found during development is recorded, and the conventional regime is afterwards given equal budget to reproduce it.

Metric
Detection rate (mutants caught ÷ mutants injected) and time-to-first-failure, in CPU-seconds, per regime.
Success
A statistically defensible difference in either metric, reported honestly in whichever direction it falls.

RQ3What does strong consistency cost?

Throughput and latency against etcd v3 on identical hardware, at cluster sizes three and five, under YCSB workloads A (update-heavy), B (read-heavy), and C (read-only), across value sizes from 64 B to 64 KB. Verity is additionally measured with batching and pipelining disabled, and across all three read strategies, to attribute cost to specific mechanisms rather than reporting one aggregate number.

Metric
Operations per second; p50 / p99 / p99.9 latency; CPU and fsync counts per operation.
Success
Within one order of magnitude of etcd, with every gap attributed to a named, understood mechanism. Beating etcd is not an objective; explaining the difference is.

RQ4How available is the system during failure and reconfiguration?

At least 500 leader kills are injected at uniformly random points, and the client-observed unavailability window is measured for each. The same measurement is taken across a rolling restart of every node, and across shard migration under continuous write load.

Metric
Distribution of failover time (mean, p99, max); client error rate and unavailability window during migration and rolling restart.
Success
Failover bounded by the election timeout configuration in the overwhelming majority of cases, with every outlier explained.

RQ5Are leader-lease reads actually safe?

Lease-based reads are correct only under an assumption about clock drift. The simulator injects clock skew from zero upward, in both directions, and both read strategies are run against the same seeds and the same checker.

Metric
Linearizability violation rate as a function of injected skew, for ReadIndex reads and lease reads.
Success
A measured skew threshold at which lease reads begin to violate linearizability, with ReadIndex clean at every skew tested.

RQ5 deserves a note on why it is in the plan. It is small, it is cheap, and it produces a result that is concrete, slightly surprising, and impossible to obtain any other way — which makes it the clearest single demonstration that the simulator earns its cost.

The defence demonstration

A real bug from the catalogue is reproduced live from its seed in a few seconds, the failing history is shown with the violating operation highlighted by the checker, the one-line fix is applied, and the same seed is re-run clean. Reproducing a distributed-systems failure on demand, in front of an audience, is something most practising engineers cannot do — and it takes under two minutes.

Technical write-up for a general engineering audience, published publicly. Optional, and worth doing — this work is of interest well beyond the examination.
